The Side Hustle Math
Nobody Talks About

$25/hour sounds great until you factor in gas, taxes, and wear on your car. Here's how to calculate what you're really making.

An ad that quotes an hourly rate is talking about gross, and sometimes about a busy hour rather than a week. The number you can spend is what remains after the costs of the work, the hours you were not paid, and the tax you will actually owe.

A fully labeled example, with every dollar marked as assumed, is on the side-gig worksheet. Use that page with your own payout, your own miles, and the mileage rate the IRS is publishing this year. Do not reuse a rate from an old article. This one will not give you a fake “typical shift.”

The lines that change the hourly rate

1. Unpaid time. Waiting, driving to the zone, and the notes you write afterward are hours. Divide by all of them, not only by the hours the app called “active.”

2. The vehicle. Fuel, tires, and repairs are the gig’s cost if the gig required the miles. The IRS publishes a standard mileage rate and changes it. Use the current one, or use your own cost per mile if you know it. Do not subtract fuel a second time if the rate you chose already includes fuel.

3. Insurance. A personal auto policy may not cover a delivery or rideshare crash. Ask your insurer what your policy does. The price of a commercial endorsement is whatever they quote you, not a number from this page.

4. Tax set-aside. Federal self-employment tax is the Social Security and Medicare tax on net self-employment earnings (the IRS describes the combined rate as 15.3 percent, before additional Medicare tax and before income tax). Income tax is separate. Apply both to profit after costs, not to the gross payout, and treat this paragraph as a map rather than a return. It is not tax advice.

When Side Hustles Actually Make Sense

Not all gigs are bad. Some legitimately pay well when you run the real numbers:

Work with low extra cost: Writing, design, tutoring, or anything you can do without burning miles. The take-home is closer to the quoted rate because there is less to subtract. The quoted rate still has to be a rate you have actually been paid.

Asset-light services: Dog walking, tutoring, consulting — minimal vehicle wear, no inventory costs, just your time and expertise.

Scale-up potential: Selling digital products, building a course, creating content — upfront work, but revenue keeps coming without linear time investment.

How to Actually Calculate Your Rate

Here's the formula:

Real Hourly Rate =
(Gross Earnings - Expenses - Taxes) ÷ Total Time Invested

Expenses to include:

  • Mileage (use $0.67/mile or track actual gas + maintenance)
  • Platform fees, read from your own payout. The cut differs by company and it changes. Do not use a number from an article if your statement shows a different one.
  • Supplies (bags, phone mount, etc.)
  • Additional insurance if required

Taxes to include:

  • Self-employment tax: 15.3% on net income
  • Federal income tax: 10-37% depending on bracket
  • State income tax: 0-13% depending on state

Time to include:

  • Active work time
  • Wait time between gigs
  • Travel to/from work zones
  • Administrative tasks (tracking expenses, filing taxes, etc.)

Compare it to a job you would actually take

After costs and a tax set-aside, compare the hourly figure to a wage you could earn in those same hours. There is no universal cutoff on this site. A W-2 job also withholds taxes and may not ask you to burn miles. Put that job’s wage next to your net hourly number and decide. The worksheet shows the division with an example whose dollars are labeled as assumed.
