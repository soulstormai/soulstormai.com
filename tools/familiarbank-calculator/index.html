Household money · Method

How to read a household budget without a fake “average family”

A budget is a list of money in and money out for a period you choose, usually a month. The useful result is not a grade. It is three numbers you can check against the next month: what came in, what left, and what remained.

Open the FamiliarBank calculator

That calculator is at familiarbank.soulstormai.com. This page is the method, so the calculator has something honest to calculate. SoulstormAI does not see the numbers you type there.

Use one month, and use take-home

Pick a month that already happened. Future months are guesses. Take-home pay is what reached the bank after withholding. If you are self-employed, use what you actually transferred to yourself, and read the side-gig worksheet before you treat a payout as income.

If income changes every month, write three months side by side and budget from the smaller one. The larger months are not a salary. They are variance.

Sort spending into two piles

Fixed is anything with a due date and an amount you already know: rent or mortgage, insurance premiums, loan minimums, childcare that is contracted, utilities on a level plan. List the minimum you must pay to stay current. Extra principal is a choice, so put it with savings, not with the bill.

Variable is everything else: groceries, fuel, eating out, clothes, gifts, medical bills that are not a premium, subscriptions you could cancel. Go through a statement. Memory undercounts restaurants and delivery.

A third pile, if you want it, is irregular: car registration, annual dues, gifts you know are coming. Divide the yearly amount by 12 and treat that twelfth as a monthly fixed cost. Otherwise those bills look like emergencies when they are on the calendar.

The only ratio this page will defend

Savings for the month = take-home − fixed − variable. Savings rate = that remainder ÷ take-home.

If the remainder is negative, the rate is negative. That is the sentence the budget is for. It does not mean you failed a national benchmark. It means the month spent more than it brought in, and the next question is which line you can change.

There is no target percent on this site. A household supporting a parent, paying a medical bill, or in a high-rent city is not the same problem as a household that is not. Compare yourself to your previous month.

An example with invented dollars

The dollars are made up so the ratio is easy to see. Do not treat them as a recommended budget.

Take-home: $5,400.

Fixed: rent $1,800, insurance $220, car payment $310, childcare $900, phones $80. Fixed total $3,310.

Variable: groceries $650, fuel $160, eating out $240, everything else $280. Variable total $1,330.

Remainder: 5,400 − 3,310 − 1,330 = $760.

Savings rate: 760 ÷ 5,400 = about 14% of take-home that month.

If childcare is about to rise by $200, the remainder becomes $560 and the rate falls, with no change in “discipline.” The line changed. That is the point of writing the lines down.

What to ignore

  • A pie chart of “what families spend” that is not your statement.
  • A rule that rent must be a certain percent, unless a landlord or a loan officer is actually applying that rule to you. Then use their rule, and still list the other bills.
  • Categories so fine that you stop updating them. Groceries and restaurants can be two lines. Twelve kinds of groceries cannot, unless you enjoy bookkeeping.

When the list is honest for one month, take it to the FamiliarBank calculator if you want the arithmetic in a form you can change quickly. If a number on that other site disagrees with this worksheet, trust the worksheet you can explain.
